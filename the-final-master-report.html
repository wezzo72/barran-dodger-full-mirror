2. Evidence classification

This report applies the classification system used in the archive.

Code	Classification	Meaning
A	Documented fact	Directly supported by a reliable primary record or authoritative determination
B	Corroborated claim	Supported by materially independent or corroborating evidence
C	Documented allegation	Recorded in a complaint, statement or correspondence but not independently established
D	Inference	Analytical conclusion drawn from documented circumstances
E	Interpretation	Legal, philosophical, psychological, political or theological interpretation
F	Unresolved	Evidence is incomplete, conflicting or insufficient for a stronger conclusion

A source must not be used to prove more than it establishes.

A complaint establishes that a complaint was made. A government acknowledgement establishes that correspondence was received or recorded. A court filing establishes that a filing occurred. A formal determination establishes what the relevant authority actually determined.

A cryptographic timestamp may establish that a hash existed at a particular time. It does not establish that every statement inside the document is true.

This distinction protects the archive from exaggeration and protects the author from having legitimate documentary evidence dismissed because some allegations remain unresolved.

PART V — THE ARCHITECTURE OF ADMINISTRATIVE ERASURE

10. The cumulative-consequence thesis

The author’s central analytical concept is the Architecture of Administrative Erasure.

This term describes the author’s concern that a sequence of administrative classifications, decisions, referrals, restrictions, refusals and omissions may have interacted to reduce his practical ability to exercise rights and independence.

The report does not assume that a coordinated architecture existed.

It tests the proposition.

The distinction is important because cumulative harm can occur even where separate institutions acted independently.

A person may experience a chain of adverse consequences without there being a single central actor or common instruction.

The evidence must therefore be examined at two levels:

Individual decision: What happened, who made it, what authority was relied upon and what consequence followed?

Cumulative consequence: How did that decision interact with other decisions to affect housing, income, mobility, support, safety and access to remedies?

The administrative mechanisms requiring examination

Classification: What status or category was assigned to the author, and what effect did it have?

Information flow: What did each institution actually receive, and which decision-makers knew the relevant information?

Referral chains: Was the matter transferred between agencies? What was transferred, and what action followed?

Repeated non-allocation: Were complaints repeatedly declined without a substantive examination of the underlying issue?

Missing records: What records are absent, who should have held them, and what searches were conducted?

Health and administration: Were clinical decisions and administrative decisions kept properly distinct?

Financial administration: Did restrictions on money or assets materially reduce practical independence?

Housing and support: Did the combination of accommodation and support arrangements leave the author without a realistic alternative?

Professional and personal consequences: What losses are documented, and what causal relationship is established?

The coordination test

A coordinated scheme requires connecting evidence.

Potentially relevant evidence includes direct communications, shared instructions, common records, synchronized actions, admissions or explicit cross-references between decision-makers.

Temporal proximity alone is insufficient.

Provisional finding: The archive provides a basis to examine cumulative administrative consequences. It does not presently establish a single coordinated scheme as a proven fact.

PART IX — CONTRADICTIONS AND UNANSWERED QUESTIONS

23. Contradiction register

The archive’s Report 5 identifies the need to compare competing records rather than simply accumulate documents supporting one conclusion.

The principal discrepancy categories are:

Repository and document counts

Different counts may reflect different dates, repositories, file types, duplicates and inventory methods.

They must not be presented as one consistent total without reconciliation.

Official records and personal accounts

A personal statement may describe an event differently from an institutional record.

The discrepancy must be identified, dated and tested.

Legal classifications

A disclosure, complaint, administrative assessment, non-allocation decision and judicial determination are different things.

They must not be described interchangeably.

Clinical records

Different diagnoses or clinical descriptions must be compared by clinician, date, purpose and context.

Financial records

Financial totals must be reproducible from source documents.

Institutional knowledge

Sending a document to an institution does not automatically establish that every later decision-maker read it.

Receipt, acknowledgement, forwarding and actual knowledge must be distinguished.

Missing records

A missing record does not automatically prove destruction.

The investigator must establish who should have held it, what searches were undertaken, applicable retention requirements and whether another document confirms its existence.

24. Alternative explanations

A proper investigation must test explanations that may weaken the author’s preferred interpretation.

These may include:

* Independent administrative decisions.
* Jurisdictional limits.
* Incomplete information.
* Administrative error.
* Different decision-makers applying different criteria.
* Resource constraints.
* Independent clinical judgments.
* Lawful exercises of discretion.
* Unrelated events.
* Poor communication without deliberate coordination.
* Actual misconduct or negligence.

The existence of alternative explanations does not establish institutional innocence.

It is part of the evidentiary process.
