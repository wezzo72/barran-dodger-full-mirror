5. The decisive procedural issue

He was not satisfied that the disclosure related to the Federal Court, Federal Circuit and Family Court, or National Native Title Tribunal.

He therefore concluded that the correspondence had not been provided to an authorised recipient for an internal disclosure under the Act.

6. Decision

He decided not to allocate the handling of the disclosure to any agency.

He stated that no further action under the PID Act would be taken by the Federal Court or FCFCOA, or another Commonwealth agency, on that correspondence.

7. Notification

He also stated that he would not advise the Principal Officers of the Federal Court or FCFCOA of the disclosure or his decision.

Why this document matters

It is an actual institutional assessment, not merely an acknowledgement.

It records the nature of the concerns, recognises the possibility that the alleged conduct could be disclosable, and explains why the Federal Court did not progress the matter under the PID Act.

It does not determine whether the underlying allegations were true or false, and it does not establish that another agency was legally required to investigate them.

That distinction is essential to an impartial examination.

5. THE EVIDENTIARY POSITION SO FAR

The strongest documentary result is the Federal Court assessment.

The APRA/OAIC and NDIS records now have identifiable filenames and can be targeted precisely rather than searched for blindly.

The next examination should place each disclosure beside the institution’s actual response and record:

  1. Date and recipient of the original disclosure.
  2. The conduct reported.
  3. The agency’s acknowledgement or decision.
  4. The statutory or procedural reason given.
  5. Whether the matter was referred, allocated, declined or closed.
  6. Any later complaint or review.
  7. Any subsequent event, without assuming that chronology alone proves retaliation.

The distinction between documentary fact and inference

A complete forensic examination must distinguish:

  • Documentary fact: What the original record actually says.
  • Official record: What an agency formally recorded or decided.
  • Corroborated evidence: What is independently supported by other records.
  • Testimony: What the person involved reports experiencing.
  • Inference: What may reasonably be concluded from the sequence of events.
  • Allegation: What remains asserted but not established.
  • Interpretation or faith: Personal meaning and spiritual understanding, which must not be represented as documentary proof.

The fact that an agency declined to allocate or progress a disclosure does not, by itself, prove retaliation, conspiracy or wrongdoing. Equally, an agency’s decision not to progress a disclosure does not establish that the underlying concerns were false.

The documentary record must be examined on its own terms.
