The central distinction throughout this work is between:

Documented fact: what an identifiable primary-source document actually records.

Official record: what a government agency, court, tribunal or other authorised body has formally recorded or decided.

Author allegation: what Dr Richard William McLean or another author alleges occurred.

Inference: a conclusion drawn from a pattern of documents or events, which may require further testing.

Unverified matter: a claim for which the supporting source has not yet been independently established.

An unanswered allegation is not automatically an admission. A blockchain timestamp can support the integrity and chronology of a preserved record, but cannot independently prove the truth of the contents. An ICC submission or acknowledgement does not itself establish that the Court has accepted the allegations as proven or made a finding under the Rome Statute. Likewise, a criminal charge is not a conviction, and sentencing consequences depend on the applicable law and the eventual outcome of proceedings.

These distinctions do not diminish the importance of the archive. They make its claims more testable, its conclusions more defensible, and its demand for accountability harder to dismiss.
