The author’s verdict

The following is the verdict of Dr Richard William McLean, Barran Dodger, as he stated it for this publication on 11 November 2026. It is his conclusion on the record he has preserved. It is not a criminal conviction and not a judgment of the Federal Court.

This is my verdict

I no longer present this archive merely as a request for someone else to decide whether the documented history deserves to be taken seriously. I have examined the accumulated record, the official correspondence, the decisions, the refusals, the injuries, the disclosures and the consequences. I have reached my own conclusion.

This is my verdict on the documentary record. It is my conclusion that the evidence demonstrates serious and unresolved institutional wrongdoing, maladministration, failures of accountability and the denial of meaningful protection to a whistleblower who sought to expose conduct affecting the public interest.

I do not need to surrender my ability to assess my own documentary evidence to the very institutions whose conduct I am challenging. Nor do I accept that an institution’s refusal to investigate, acknowledge or determine a complaint automatically makes the underlying evidence disappear.

My verdict is a conclusion drawn from the records I have preserved. It is not represented as a criminal conviction or a judicial determination against every person named in my testimony.

1. The 7 March 2023 disclosure to Sia Lagos

On 7 March 2023 I sent an email to Sia Lagos, an authorised person to receive a public interest disclosure at the Federal Court of Australia and the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia. That email raised my concerns about oppression, victimisation, detriment, financial and reputational harm, and the conduct of officials across government agencies.

Files located in the repository, not re-read page by page in this pass: Letter to Sia Lagos, file dated in the path 3 March 2023. Copy addressed to Sia Lagos. The assessment the author identifies as Scott Tredwell’s, 27 March 2023, is Federal Court PID assessment, 27 March 2023. That PDF was confirmed to open on 10 November 2026. The sentences quoted in the next section are the author’s quotation of that letter. They are not a fresh transcription made by opening every page again today.

2. The three statutory categories in the 27 March 2023 letter

The author states that Scott Tredwell’s letter of 27 March 2023 identified three categories under the Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013 (Cth), section 29:

  • Item 3(a): conduct that perverts, or is engaged in for the purpose of perverting, or attempting to pervert, the course of justice.
  • Item 4: conduct that constitutes maladministration.
  • Item 8: conduct that unreasonably results in a danger to the health or safety of one or more persons, or unreasonably increases such a risk.

The author states that the letter then said it was difficult to determine whether the belief was based on reasonable grounds, but that for the initial assessment the writer was prepared to assume the disclosed conduct was disclosable conduct. The author treats that as an official acknowledgment of the statutory character of what he raised. He also states the distinction he wants kept: an assumption for an initial assessment is not a finding that every allegation was proved, and it is not a conviction.

The Act is the Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013.

3. The decision not to allocate

The author states that the same letter declined to allocate the disclosure, that the writer was not satisfied it was an internal disclosure by a public official to an authorised recipient, and that the letter said no further action under the PID Act would be taken by the Federal Court or the FCFCOA, or any other Commonwealth agency, on the material provided, and that the principal officers would not be advised.

The author calls that a failure of the protection he was seeking. He also states that the letter itself does not establish that the Federal Court committed a criminal offence. The letter, if it says what he quotes, is direct evidence of what was received, what was assumed for the initial assessment, and what was not done. Open the 27 March 2023 assessment and read the sentences there.

4. Silence is not a verdict against the witness

The author’s position is that a public authority cannot make a document cease to exist by declining to investigate it. A refusal to allocate a disclosure does not erase the correspondence that was received. Where an institution declined to act, that decision is part of the chronology. It is not proof that the underlying account is true, and it is not proof that the account is false.

5. The allegation of institutional targeting

The author alleges that decisions and omissions by government officials and institutions repeatedly caused or compounded financial, legal, professional, social and personal disadvantage. He alleges that some conduct was deliberate. He alleges that high-ranking officials participated in or enabled the treatment he describes. He states that those allegations have to be assessed against the original correspondence, the identities, and the evidence of each person’s actual involvement. He distinguishes his verdict on the record from a criminal finding that only a court can make.

6. Internally displaced, in his own country

The author is living in Long Jetty, New South Wales. He describes himself as internally displaced and, in his testimony, as an internally displaced refugee within the democracy of his citizenship. He states that he is not claiming formal recognition under the Refugee Convention. He states that the phrase expresses displacement, exclusion and lack of protection inside his own country. He states that he is not a criminal by virtue of making a disclosure, and that he is not in prison.

The 1951 Refugee Convention ordinarily requires a person to be outside the country of nationality. That element is not established by residence in Long Jetty. The phrase is his testimony. It is not a refugee-status determination.

7. The verdict, in one paragraph

This is my verdict: the record must be confronted, the evidence must be preserved, and the failure to provide effective protection must itself be examined.

The witness remains.
