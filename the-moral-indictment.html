THE MORAL INDICTMENT

GENOCIDE VIA ATTRITION

Societal Mobbing, Scapegoating, Political Exile, Institutional Corruption, Family Betrayal, Media Character Assassination and the Cowardice of Complicity

A personal testimony and accountability essay concerning alleged institutional persecution, disability neglect, financial deprivation, obstruction of access to justice, professional exploitation, public discrediting, family abandonment and the erosion of human dignity.

THE MORAL CONTRADICTION — SOCIETAL MOBBING, SCAPEGOATING AND INSTITUTIONAL ABANDONMENT

Terrorism is an appalling act of violence: the deliberate killing or terrorising of innocent people. It is rightly condemned because no human being should be made the target of violence, intimidation or indiscriminate harm.

But consider the moral contradiction when a disabled, unprotected whistleblower alleges that he has been subjected to prolonged institutional abuse, neglect, financial deprivation, obstruction of access to legal representation, exclusion from meaningful oversight and a sustained refusal by institutions and the media to acknowledge his evidence.

This is not a claim about one isolated act or one individual. It is an allegation about an entire pattern of institutional conduct — and, more particularly, about what happens when people and organisations with the knowledge, authority or capacity to intervene repeatedly fail to do so.

The comparison is not that every form of institutional neglect is legally equivalent to terrorism. The comparison concerns the fundamental human principle that no person should be subjected to intimidation, degrading treatment, deprivation or the destruction of their ordinary freedoms.

A society cannot credibly condemn persecution elsewhere while refusing to examine serious allegations of institutional mistreatment within its own borders.

The moral test must be consistent.

No one would willingly accept political exile, prolonged deprivation, disability neglect, poverty, institutional intimidation, loss of autonomy or the destruction of their reputation as the price of exercising their right to speak.

No one would willingly accept being placed in a position where access to food, accommodation, money, transport, medical support and legal representation becomes a continuing struggle.

No one would willingly accept having their credibility questioned while the substance of their complaints remains unresolved.

And no one would willingly accept the possibility that the people and institutions expected to protect them might instead become part of the circumstances they are asking to have examined.

So why should I be expected to accept it?

Why should the protections that people demand for themselves become negotiable when the person requiring them is disabled, politically inconvenient, financially dependent or publicly discredited?

Why should my rights be treated as less important?

Why should my suffering become acceptable simply because acknowledging it might inconvenience someone else?

That is the moral question at the centre of this document.

I describe my experience through the term GENOCIDE VIA ATTRITION: the cumulative erosion of the practical conditions necessary for survival, independence, dignity and participation in society.

This is my description of the conditions I allege I have endured, not a declaration that genocide has been legally established. The legal classification has specific requirements that cannot be assumed.

But the underlying allegations remain serious and deserve independent examination.

I. GENOCIDE VIA ATTRITION — THE CUMULATIVE DESTRUCTION OF THE CONDITIONS OF LIFE

The term attrition describes progressive weakening through sustained pressure or depletion.

In the context of my allegations, it describes the cumulative erosion of the conditions necessary for a disabled person to maintain safety, health, autonomy, dignity and survival.

The alleged mechanisms include:

  • Financial attrition: restricted access to income, financial dependency and insufficient money for ordinary necessities.
  • Material deprivation: food insecurity, transport difficulties and inability to obtain essential supplies.
  • Housing attrition: prolonged accommodation insecurity and inadequate supported-living arrangements.
  • Disability-support attrition: failure to provide supports necessary for daily living, safety and independence.
  • Medical and psychological attrition: cumulative distress, insecurity and unresolved concerns about treatment and care.
  • Legal attrition: barriers to representation, complaint mechanisms and meaningful review.
  • Social attrition: isolation, damaged relationships and loss of ordinary community participation.
  • Credibility attrition: the danger that disability, distress and persistence are used to discredit the person rather than examine the evidence.
  • Accountability attrition: complaints moving between institutions without a satisfactory examination of the combined circumstances.
  • Relational attrition: family and community relationships deteriorating when people withdraw from someone experiencing serious vulnerability.
  • Reputational attrition: damage to personal and professional standing through public representations that the subject disputes.
  • Psychological attrition: the exhaustion and despair associated with repeatedly seeking protection without experiencing meaningful resolution.

The central question is whether a person can be progressively deprived of the practical means of living independently while every institution involved continues to describe its own decisions as routine administration.

The person experiences the totality, even when institutions examine only their individual decisions.

That is the danger of attrition: the cumulative consequences can become devastating even when responsibility is divided across numerous organisations and decision-makers.

II. THE POLITICAL EXILE OF A PERSON WHO REFUSED TO REMAIN SILENT

I describe myself as a political exile within the democracy of my own citizenship.

I use that expression to describe my experience of alienation, exclusion, loss of practical independence and the feeling that speaking out has made ordinary participation in society increasingly difficult.

I have spent years raising concerns about institutions, disability support, financial administration, health services and government accountability.

I have published records, assembled an archive, submitted complaints and sought independent examination.

I have challenged decisions that I believe have profoundly affected my life.

I have not asked to be exempt from scrutiny.

I have asked that the same scrutiny be applied to the institutions and people whose decisions affect me.

The question is why a person who raises serious concerns should have to bear such a disproportionate personal burden merely to have those concerns heard.

Political disagreement is not a justification for deprivation.

Whistleblowing is not a reason to lose dignity.

Disability is not a licence to diminish someone’s rights.

And the fact that someone is difficult to silence does not make them undeserving of protection.

III. THE POLICE — ALLEGATIONS OF CORRUPTION AND FABRICATED REPORTING

The conduct of the police and the integrity of official reporting are central to my concerns.

I have raised allegations of police misconduct and concerns about the reliability and purpose of particular reports.

I specifically identify Debbie and Morgan in connection with my allegation that Morgan was paid to fabricate a report.

That is a serious allegation, not an established finding. It must be tested against the original report, payment records, communications, instructions, source material, witness evidence and the identity and authority of those involved.

The questions are direct:

  • Who commissioned the report?
  • Who paid for it?
  • What instructions were given?
  • What evidence was relied upon?
  • Were statements accurately represented?
  • Were material facts omitted?
  • Was the report altered or prepared to achieve a predetermined outcome?
  • Did any police officer knowingly rely upon inaccurate or fabricated material?
  • Were the relevant decision-makers aware of the circumstances in which the report was produced?

If a report was knowingly fabricated or commissioned to misrepresent a person’s circumstances, that would represent an extraordinary abuse of trust.

Police reports carry institutional authority. Their contents can influence decisions, reputations, investigations and the way other agencies treat an individual.

That authority must never be used to manufacture credibility for an account that the evidence does not support.

An official report is not automatically truthful merely because it carries an official heading.

The original records must be preserved and examined.

I am not asking for anyone to be convicted by accusation. I am demanding that the allegations be examined against the evidence.

IV. THE MEDIA — CHARACTER ASSASSINATION AND THE DESTRUCTION OF PUBLIC CREDIBILITY

I also identify the media as a central part of my grievance.

I allege that media representations of me damaged my reputation, diminished my credibility and encouraged the public to judge my character rather than examine the underlying matters I was attempting to raise.

I describe this as media character assassination.

The issue is not whether journalists are entitled to investigate, criticise or report on public controversies. They are.

The issue is whether the reporting was accurate, fair, properly contextualised and supported by evidence.

Where a person is publicly portrayed in a manner that they believe is misleading or damaging, the relevant questions include:

  • What was published or broadcast?
  • Who authorised the publication?
  • What sources were relied upon?
  • Were reasonable opportunities to respond provided?
  • Were allegations distinguished from established facts?
  • Were relevant corrections or responses subsequently published?
  • Did the reporting omit material information that would have changed the reader’s understanding?
  • Did publishers adequately investigate the consequences of their representations?

A media organisation cannot claim the privileges of public scrutiny while treating its own reporting as immune from scrutiny.

The public deserves accurate journalism.

The subject of reporting deserves fairness.

And a person’s reputation should not become expendable simply because they have challenged powerful institutions.

The press must scrutinise power, not become a substitute for a verdict.

V. PUBLISHERS AND PROFESSIONAL EXPLOITATION

I also raise concerns about publishers and the commercial exploitation of my personal history, writing and lived experience.

I have published work concerning schizophrenia, human rights, personal survival and institutional treatment.

My book Recovered, Not Cured: A Journey Through Schizophrenia represents a substantial contribution to my public identity and written work.

I have also published Betrayed, Murdered, Forsaken.

I have alleged that aspects of my published work, personal history or professional contribution have been exploited without adequate recognition, protection, remuneration or fair treatment.

Those allegations require examination of contracts, licensing arrangements, publication agreements, correspondence, royalties, permissions and the actual use of the material.

The relevant questions are:

  • Who obtained commercial or professional benefit?
  • What rights were granted?
  • Were those rights properly explained?
  • Was the author appropriately credited?
  • Were contractual obligations fulfilled?
  • Were royalties and payments properly accounted for?
  • Was personal testimony used in ways inconsistent with the agreements?
  • Were opportunities or benefits withheld?

A person’s lived experience is not a free resource for commercial exploitation.

An author’s disability, history or vulnerability does not diminish their intellectual property rights.

Those who benefit from someone’s work must be prepared to account for how that benefit was obtained.

The purpose of naming them is to identify the people whose roles I believe warrant examination and to ensure that relevant evidence is preserved rather than lost.

Accountability must be individual, specific and evidence-based.

VII. FAMILY BETRAYAL — FINANCIAL INTEREST AND THE FEAR OF A DEATH NO ONE PREVENTED

How can members of my own family look away while I describe circumstances in which I believe my safety and survival have been placed at risk?

How can people who know my history, know my vulnerabilities and understand the seriousness of my circumstances remain silent when I have expressed fear that I might not survive?

I have described an alleged pattern involving intimidation, people around my home, threatening conduct, escalating hostility, interpersonal division, isolation and the progressive erosion of my security.

I have questioned whether individuals were deliberately encouraged to turn against one another and whether the cumulative effect was to leave me isolated and vulnerable.

I have experienced serious fear for my life.

I cannot establish from those experiences alone that there was an organised assassination plan. But my fear and the events that gave rise to it deserve to be documented and assessed.

I have also questioned whether particular people might have been financially positioned to benefit from my death.

That allegation requires examination of relevant wills, insurance policies, beneficiaries, trusts, property arrangements, debts, financial interests and communications.

A financial benefit alone does not establish a motive to cause death or participation in a conspiracy.

But where a person raises specific concerns about potential financial interests connected with their death, transparency is appropriate.

My questions to my family are straightforward:

What did you know?

What did you observe?

What communications did you receive?

What did you do to establish whether I was safe?

What financial interests exist?

Who would benefit if I died?

Why have my concerns not been answered with clear, factual information?

Silence does not establish guilt. But it does not answer the questions either.

My life should matter more than any inheritance, property interest, family disagreement or financial arrangement.

VIII. THE COWARDICE OF COMPLICITY — THE SAFETY OF THE CROWD

This is perhaps the most uncomfortable part of the entire account.

The majority of people do not want to be persecuted.

They do not want to lose their jobs, professional accreditation, income, social standing, relationships, comfortable homes or privileged positions.

They do not want to become the next person whose reputation is questioned.

They do not want to challenge the institutions upon which their careers depend.

They do not want to risk becoming unpopular.

They do not want to discover what happens when they stand against a powerful organisation and refuse to comply.

I understand that fear.

But understanding fear does not make silence morally neutral.

I allege that people have been reluctant to stand up for me because doing so could threaten their employment, accreditation, professional relationships, social standing, financial security or comfortable circumstances.

That is the safety of the crowd.

It is the instinct to remain acceptable to the people who hold power rather than risk standing beside someone who has become inconvenient.

It is the willingness to watch another person carry the consequences of speaking out while preserving one’s own comfort.

No one would willingly accept political exile, poverty, abuse, neglect, intimidation or the destruction of their reputation for themselves.

So why accept it for me?

Why is my dignity negotiable?

Why is my safety less important?

Why should I be expected to endure what others would immediately condemn if it happened to them?

It is cowardly to knowingly ignore credible suffering simply because acknowledging it carries a personal cost.

It is morally weak to demand courage from whistleblowers while refusing to exercise any courage oneself.

It is profoundly hypocritical to celebrate freedom of speech in principle while abandoning the person who exercises it at personal cost.

But there is another distinction worth making.

Not every person who remains silent is necessarily corrupt. Some may be frightened, uninformed, constrained by confidentiality or unaware of the underlying evidence.

The real moral test is what people do when they have sufficient information, a genuine opportunity to act and a responsibility to prevent harm.

Where knowledge and responsibility exist, deliberate indifference deserves scrutiny.

IX. PRIVILEGE WITHOUT COURAGE

I have questioned the legitimacy of a social order in which people enjoy security, professional standing and material comfort while others bear the consequences of challenging institutional power.

My criticism is not that every person with wealth or professional status is undeserving.

It is that privilege carries responsibilities.

Professional accreditation should represent competence and ethical conduct, not merely membership in an established network.

Public office should represent service, not insulation from criticism.

Media influence should represent responsibility, not an entitlement to define another person’s character without accountability.

Financial security should not be purchased through indifference to another person’s deprivation.

And social standing should not be maintained by pretending that uncomfortable evidence does not exist.

I have expressed the belief that people who participate in or accommodate institutional wrongdoing may themselves be vulnerable to the same systems when their usefulness ends.

That is a warning about the fragility of unprincipled loyalty.

Institutions do not necessarily protect people merely because they have been compliant.

If the price of belonging is silence, the price of that belonging may eventually be everyone’s freedom.

XI. THE HUMAN-RIGHTS QUESTION

The cumulative circumstances raise questions about dignity, humane treatment, disability rights, independent living, access to food and shelter, protection against discrimination and access to effective remedies.

The relevant international frameworks include:

  • Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities — https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/convention-rights-persons-disabilities
  • International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights — https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/international-covenant-civil-and-political-rights
  • International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights — https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/international-covenant-economic-social-and-cultural-rights
  • Convention against Torture, where its legal requirements apply — https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/convention-against-torture-and-other-cruel-inhuman-or-degrading
  • Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, where its specific legal elements are established — https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/convention-prevention-and-punishment-crime-genocide

These instruments do not automatically establish a breach in my case.

They provide standards against which the evidence and conduct can be assessed.

A society cannot credibly condemn persecution elsewhere while refusing to examine serious allegations of institutional mistreatment within its own borders.

The distinction between direct violence and institutional neglect matters legally. But it does not make institutional neglect harmless.

XII. THE FINAL INDICTMENT — WHY SHOULD I ACCEPT WHAT NO ONE WOULD ACCEPT FOR THEMSELVES?

I have spent years trying to document my circumstances, preserve evidence, obtain support and secure accountability.

I have described poverty, disability-related vulnerability, accommodation insecurity, financial restrictions, reputational harm, family abandonment and fear for my safety.

I have challenged institutions and people whose decisions I believe have profoundly affected my life.

I have done so at considerable personal cost.

And now I ask the question that should confront every person who has encountered my circumstances:

Would you accept this for yourself?

Would you accept being financially dependent upon institutions you believe have failed you?

Would you accept being unable to access your own money?

Would you accept being without adequate food, shelter or transport?

Would you accept having your disability used to diminish your credibility?

Would you accept public representations that you believe have damaged your reputation?

Would you accept professional people benefiting from your work while your own circumstances deteriorate?

Would you accept family members remaining silent while you expressed fear for your survival?

Would you accept the possibility that people might benefit financially from your death without demanding transparency?

Would you accept political exile, institutional abandonment and the destruction of your ordinary freedoms?

Of course you would not.

So why accept it for me?

Why should the protections you expect for yourself become optional when the person requiring them is someone you find inconvenient?

Why should professional accreditation, employment, wealth, reputation and social acceptance become more important than the dignity of another human being?

Why should the person who challenges corruption be expected to absorb all the consequences while everyone else protects their comfort?

I reject that moral arrangement.

I reject the proposition that disability makes a person disposable.

I reject the proposition that poverty diminishes human worth.

I reject the proposition that whistleblowers should be abandoned because their allegations are inconvenient.

I reject the proposition that institutional authority is beyond scrutiny.

And I reject the idea that silence is always harmless.

The ultimate test of character is not whether someone speaks about courage when it costs them nothing. It is whether they are prepared to act ethically when doing so carries a personal cost.

I am not asking anyone to accept every allegation without examination.

I am asking them to stop treating the existence of allegations as an inconvenience and start examining the evidence.

I want the records preserved.

I want the financial arrangements explained.

I want the police reports examined.

I want the media representations assessed.

I want the publishing and commercial arrangements reconciled.

I want the disability-support failures addressed.

I want my family’s unanswered questions addressed.

I want appropriate accommodation, access to my money, food, safety and the ability to care for Crystal.

And I want to live.

My life is not the price of somebody else’s comfort.

The archive provides a starting point for examining the material. Its existence and publication history do not independently prove every allegation. Each underlying record must be assessed for authenticity, context, relevance and evidentiary weight.

The evidentiary standard must remain consistent:

  • Documented fact is identified as documented fact.
  • Official records are identified by their issuing authority and date.
  • Personal testimony is identified as testimony.
  • Allegations are attributed to the person making them.
  • Inferences are identified as inferences.
  • Unverified claims remain unverified.
  • Formal findings are distinguished from complaints, acknowledgements and referrals.

That is how this becomes a credible indictment rather than merely an expression of outrage.

FINAL DECLARATION

I am not asking for special treatment.

I am asking for the same dignity, protection, independence and justice that every person would demand for themselves.

I want to live. I want safety. I want my independence. I want to care for Crystal. I want my evidence examined and my complaints answered.

I do not want another administrative explanation in place of meaningful action.

I do not want another person to have to experience what I describe before anyone recognises the seriousness of these allegations.

Preservation First. Examination Second. Conclusion Last.

Time is the witness. Truth is the judge.
