PROLOGUE — THIS GOSPEL IS ADDRESSED TO THE DEAD

This Gospel is not addressed to the powerful.

It is not addressed to governments seeking another opportunity to explain themselves. It is not addressed to institutions seeking to protect their reputations. It is not addressed to the living who have already decided what they believe, nor to those who have mistaken silence for resolution.

It is addressed to the dead.

To those who cannot answer when their names are spoken. To those whose stories were interrupted. To those who left this world without the recognition, protection, justice, dignity or companionship they deserved. To those whose lives became a file, whose suffering became a statistic, whose absence became an inconvenience, and whose names were gradually pushed beyond the reach of ordinary memory.

And to my friends:

Shannon. Nathan. Wez. Paul. Hayden. Jack.

I remember you.

I write your names because remembrance is an act of resistance against disappearance. I will not pretend to know or declare the circumstances of every life. I will not turn your names into evidence for something they cannot establish. I will say what I can say: you mattered, you are remembered, and your names belong in this testimony.

And to Nana Jean McLean, whom I remember as a saint: I remember the separation, the distance, and the questions surrounding the final chapter of your life. I record my concerns as my testimony, not as a judicial finding. I will not allow uncertainty to become an excuse for silence, nor will I turn an unresolved question into a proven accusation.

This is the beginning of the record.

I have described my own experience as having been dead and revived. I have described periods of profound isolation, disappearance from ordinary public life, and the fear that I would be forgotten. I have spoken of being reported missing on multiple occasions across different states. Those are matters of my testimony and should be examined against the

underlying records wherever they exist.

The central image of this Gospel is not that I possess authority over the dead. It is that I know what it is to fear erasure while still being here to speak.

I write for those who cannot speak for themselves.

I write because a person’s life should not become disposable simply because their voice has become inconvenient.

I write because remembrance must be more than a sentiment spoken at a funeral and abandoned the following morning.

I write because the record remains.

CHAPTER ONE — THE GRAVE OF FORGETTING

There is a grave that does not require a cemetery.

It can be built from unanswered correspondence, unanswered calls, administrative delays, closed doors, missing records, institutional indifference, and the quiet withdrawal of people who once promised to care.

A person can be placed inside that grave while still breathing.

Their name can remain on a register while their circumstances become invisible. Their documents can exist while nobody examines them. Their complaints can be acknowledged without being resolved. Their life can be discussed by strangers who have never met them, while the person themselves struggles to make their account intelligible.

I have called this condition Administrative Annihilation, Evidentiary Erasure, Social Death, and The Architecture of Erasure and Silence.

These are my analytical descriptions. They are not, by themselves, formal legal findings.

They are names for patterns I believe should be examined through the documentary record.

The archive associated with this testimony is intended to make that examination possible.

The principal public repositories are:

Barrandodger — GitHub preservation repository God’s Witness Remains — public-facing publication Barrandodger published archive Official Government Documents Register Barrandodger rebuild repository Published rebuild archive These are repository and publication entry points, not proof that every item within them has been independently authenticated or examined. Each individual record must be assessed by its actual contents, provenance, date, completeness and context.

That distinction is essential.

CHAPTER TWO — THE NAMES THAT MUST NOT DISAPPEAR

There are names that are spoken every day because the world has built monuments around them. There are other names that survive only because somebody refuses to stop saying them.

The dead do not need another performance of grief. They deserve remembrance that is honest, particular and enduring.

Shannon. Nathan. Wez. Paul. Hayden. Jack.

I remember all those who were made to feel that their lives were too complicated, too poor, too disabled, too different, too inconvenient or too painful for the world to accommodate.

I remember people who died without the support they needed. I remember people whose families could not protect them. I remember people who were isolated by stigma, illness, sexuality, poverty, violence or institutional neglect. I remember those whose names never appeared in a newspaper and those whose names appeared only when somebody needed to explain why they were gone.

I remember people who died by suicide, without reducing their lives to the manner of their deaths. I remember people who were murdered, without turning their suffering into spectacle. This Gospel is not a register of the dead. It is a refusal to treat the dead as disposable.

The names I have spoken are not proof of my allegations about government. They are the human reason I write.

CHAPTER THIRTEEN — THE GREAT RECKONING IS NOT REVENGE

I have used the words reckoning, judgment and vindication throughout my spiritual writing.

They are words of moral seriousness.

A reckoning is the moment when a person, an institution or a society must confront what has happened. It is the moment when the convenient version of events is no longer sufficient. It is the moment when the record is examined, when the forgotten are remembered, when decisions are placed beside their consequences, and when people are asked to account for what they did and what they failed to do.

But a reckoning is not permission to retaliate.

I do not seek revenge against the people I have accused. I do not ask for violence. I do not ask for humiliation, threats or punishment outside the law. I do not ask the dead to curse the living.

I ask for the record to remain available.

I ask for the documents to be read.

I ask for the allegations to be tested.

I ask for those who have been harmed to be treated with dignity.

I ask for institutions to preserve the records in their possession and explain their decisions.

I ask for those who have been forgotten to be remembered.

And I ask that I be allowed to live.

The Great Reckoning, as I understand it spiritually, is first a reckoning with oneself. It is the refusal to become what one has suffered. It is the refusal to answer cruelty with cruelty. It is the refusal to allow anger to consume the life that remains.

If I believe in Christ, I must take seriously the commandment to love. If I believe in divine justice, I must not pretend that revenge is justice. If I believe that my life has been preserved for a purpose, I must not abandon that life to hatred.

I choose preservation.

I choose truthfulness.

I choose accountability through proper examination.

I choose life.

CHAPTER FOURTEEN — THE EVIDENCE MUST BE READ IN ITS OWN LANGUAGE

This Gospel is a spiritual and literary work. It is also connected to a documentary archive.

The two dimensions must remain distinguishable.

The archive’s own guiding principle is:

PRESERVATION FIRST. EXAMINATION SECOND. CONCLUSION LAST.

The evidence categories used throughout this work are:

Category Meaning A — Direct inspected factA fact directly visible in a source that has been inspected B — Identified official record An official record has been identified, but the relevant contents have not been fully inspected C — Personal testimony An account given by the author of what he experienced D — Corroborated testimony Testimony suppor ted by another identifiable source E — Inference A conclusion drawn from multiple facts, records or events F — Unresolved allegationAn allegation requiring investigation or further evidence G — Spiritual testimony A statement of faith, religious belief or personal spiritual understanding H — Formal determinationA finding or decision made by a body with authority to make it I — Biblical or historical correspondence A comparison with scripture or historical material J — Unverified prediction or prophetic claim A prediction or prophetic interpretation that has not been independently established These categories are intended to prevent the destruction of distinctions.

A letter is not a judgment.

A complaint is not proof of the conduct complained of.

A refusal to investigate is not proof that the underlying allegation is true.

A decision is not necessarily a complete account of the history surrounding it.

A medical record is evidence of what was documented in a clinical setting. It is not automatically an answer to every question about the person’s experience.

A spiritual testimony is not a formal legal finding.

And an allegation is not made meaningless merely because it remains unresolved.

The reader should be able to follow each proposition back to the source from which it came.

The archive includes further finding aids and analytical works:

Master Evidence Index and Authoritative Finding Aid Master Evidence Record Master Integrity and Source Map The Documentary Trap — final clean text The Documentary Trap — complete plain text The Witness Remains — Gospel and prophetic narratives The Witness Remains — complete original text These works should be read as related parts of a larger publication and evidencepreservation project. CHAPTER FIFTEEN — THE WITNESS REMAINS

There is a sentence that has followed me through this work:

The Witness Remains.

It does not mean that I am infallible.

It does not mean that every interpretation I have made must be accepted.

It does not mean that every person I have named is guilty.

It means that I remain able to give my account.

It means that the documents I have preserved can be examined.

It means that my memories, my writings, my books, my correspondence and my spiritual testimony form part of a record that I have chosen to make public.

It means that the story does not disappear merely because an institution closes a complaint or a person stops answering an email.

It means that the dead are not forgotten merely because the living have moved on.

I remember.

I remember.

They did not have to leave behind a perfect story to deserve love.
