A declaration of human dignity, ethical responsibility and accountability

Dr Barran Dodger — Dr Richard William McLean

This public testimony has two parts. First, a definitive statement of the ethical standards by which human conduct and institutional responsibility must be judged. Second, an examination of the significant people in the testimony, including political figures, executives, paid support workers and administrative decision-makers.

Nobody receives automatic exoneration because of their position. Nobody receives an automatic finding of guilt because their name appears in this testimony. Their actual conduct, decisions, communications and responsibilities must be examined against the evidence.

The same words are kept on their own permanent page at Human dignity — the accountability declaration.

The fundamental test of human integrity

Healthy, well-adjusted people do not gang up on vulnerable people, exploit disabilities, manipulate personal circumstances, manufacture narratives to discredit witnesses, betray trust, intimidate people into silence, isolate them from support or exploit positions of authority.

They do not turn a blind eye to suffering, punish people for speaking out, weaponise bureaucratic processes, conceal mistakes to protect reputations, disregard inconvenient evidence, obstruct legitimate complaints or place institutional loyalty, personal interests and financial incentives above another human being’s safety, dignity and fundamental rights.

They do not deliberately compound another person’s trauma, strip them of autonomy, undermine their credibility without examining the available evidence or treat legitimate requests for assistance as an inconvenience.

They understand that vulnerability is not consent, compliance is not necessarily agreement, silence is not proof of satisfaction and disability does not diminish a person’s credibility or entitlement to justice.

They do not exploit a person’s dependence on accommodation, medication, financial assistance, community support, healthcare or protection as leverage over their behaviour, testimony or willingness to challenge decisions they believe are unjust.

They do not coordinate to isolate someone from support, manipulate records to create misleading impressions, withhold relevant information to prevent accountability or use professional positions to evade scrutiny.

They do not retaliate against whistleblowers, discourage legitimate complaints to protect institutional reputations, dismiss documented concerns without proper examination or prioritise their own positions over the safety of people entrusted to their care.

They do not falsify records, fabricate allegations, collude to conceal wrongdoing or accept payment to deliberately harm another person.

They do not deliberately create circumstances in which someone becomes increasingly distressed and subsequently use that distress, without investigating its causes, as evidence against that person’s credibility.

They do not judge the reactions of a person experiencing prolonged adversity while ignoring the circumstances that may have produced those reactions.

They do not demand perfect communication from someone living with disability, trauma, fear or prolonged institutional conflict while refusing to provide reasonable adjustments, practical assistance and procedural fairness.

They do not mistake persistence for proof that a complaint is false, anger for proof that evidence is invalid, distress for proof that testimony is worthless or the refusal to remain silent for proof that someone deserves punishment.

They do not assume professional authority guarantees integrity, institutional reputation guarantees truth, or an official record is necessarily complete or accurate.

They understand that records can be incomplete, accounts can conflict, mistakes can occur and allegations require examination against the available evidence.

They do not demand unquestioning trust while refusing transparency. They do not expect a vulnerable person to accept decisions profoundly affecting their life without explanation, consultation or an opportunity to challenge errors.

They understand that accountability is not hostility, scrutiny is not persecution and asking difficult questions is not misconduct.

They do not abandon ethical responsibilities because another person is difficult to understand, emotionally distressed, distrustful of institutions or unwilling to accept explanations that fail to address the evidence.

Professional responsibility matters most when circumstances become difficult, power is unequal and the consequences of failure are greatest.

They do not use a person’s history, diagnosis, disability or previous experiences of crisis as a substitute for investigating a present complaint.

They do not allow assumptions about mental illness to predetermine the credibility of a witness. Nor do they dismiss documentary evidence because the person presenting it is vulnerable, unpopular or perceived as difficult.

They do not treat access to justice, healthcare, safe accommodation, financial autonomy, disability support and basic human dignity as privileges that can be withdrawn whenever someone challenges authority.

They do not make another person fight indefinitely for recognition of their humanity.

They do not force someone to repeat traumatic experiences unnecessarily, navigate fragmented systems without assistance or bear the entire burden of proving their suffering deserves attention when relevant records and decisions are held by the very institutions being asked to investigate themselves.

They understand that fairness requires neither automatic condemnation nor automatic exoneration.

It requires the willingness to discover what happened.

The accountability principle

The fundamental question is not whether someone is powerful, professionally respected, institutionally protected or personally convinced of their innocence.

The question is whether their conduct withstands objective examination against the available evidence, their professional responsibilities, applicable ethical obligations, relevant legal standards and the fundamental principles of human dignity.

Where there is evidence of misconduct, it must be examined.

Where there are inconsistencies, they must be investigated.

Where allegations cannot be substantiated, that limitation must be acknowledged.

Where wrongdoing is established, accountability must follow.

Where a vulnerable person has been failed, their need for safety, support, dignity and a fair hearing must not be conditional on their ability to withstand further suffering.

No person should have to be perfectly composed, universally liked, professionally influential or institutionally powerful to deserve protection from mistreatment.

Disability is not a licence to exploit.

Vulnerability is not consent.

Distress is not proof of dishonesty.

Speaking out is not evidence of wrongdoing.

Professional authority is not proof of integrity.

An official record is not proof of accuracy.

And institutional reputation is not a substitute for accountability.

My testimony and the people whose conduct requires examination

This declaration is not an invitation to make indiscriminate accusations. It is a demand that the people and institutions whose decisions have affected my life be examined according to the same standards of evidence, transparency and accountability that they expect of me.

I identify political figures, public decision-makers, executives, support coordinators, disability workers, financial administrators and other individuals because their positions, communications or actions form part of my personal testimony and the documentary history I have preserved.

Their responsibilities are not interchangeable.

Nor should their individual conduct be obscured by the collective reputation of an organisation.

A person who made a decision must answer for that decision. A person who administered a service must answer for their responsibilities. A person who received a complaint must answer for its handling. A person with executive authority must be accountable for the decisions within their authority.

My testimony must be examined in its entirety, not selectively.

The ultimate test

The ultimate test of integrity is not what people claim about themselves, what institutions say about their own performance or how convincingly a narrative is presented.

It is whether their actions, decisions, communications and documented conduct withstand impartial scrutiny.

Evidence must be examined before conclusions are drawn.

Authority must not be mistaken for truth.

Allegations must not be mistaken for established facts.

Testimony must not be dismissed because it challenges powerful interests.

And no human being should be deprived of dignity, safety or a fair hearing simply because their testimony is difficult for others to accept.

Preservation first. Examination second. Conclusion last.

Time is the witness. Truth is the judge.

1. Purpose and evidentiary standard

This report identifies significant individuals whose actions, decisions, professional responsibilities or communications form part of my testimony.

It considers how I say I was treated, the significance of each person’s involvement and the documentary material that needs to be examined.

The report distinguishes:

  • Documented material: the existence of identified correspondence, recordings, administrative records or other documents.
  • Personal testimony: my account of an event or interaction.
  • Allegation: conduct I attribute to an individual that requires examination against the available evidence.
  • Reasonable question: an issue arising from the chronology, responsibilities or available records.
  • Established finding: a conclusion supported by an appropriate independent determination or sufficiently established primary evidence.

The inclusion of a person does not itself establish misconduct. Equally, the absence of an official finding does not mean that an allegation should be dismissed without investigation.

Evidentiary qualification kept with this statement

The report deliberately retains the people identified, but it does not pretend that every recording has just been authenticated or that every underlying PDF in the GitHub repository has just been re-read for this page. In particular, the political figures and some of the named workers need individual source-document examinations before attributing specific decisions or misconduct to them.

The distinction is retained between the person who received a complaint, the person who made a decision, the person who implemented it and the person who had executive responsibility. That distinction is important when establishing accountability.

The next substantive step is a fully sourced people-by-people evidence register, with each person’s account linked to the actual dated correspondence, recording, PDF and relevant archive entry. The files named above are the start of that register. A filename is the route to the file. It is not, by itself, the whole of what the file says.
