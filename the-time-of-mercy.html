Prologue — the question that every human being must confront

There is a question at the centre of this testimony.

It is not a question of political allegiance.

It is not a question of professional qualifications.

It is not a question of institutional reputation, financial privilege, social standing or legal authority.

It is a question of humanity.

Can you look at another human being, recognise that they are a human being deserving of the same care, kindness, dignity and consideration that you expect for yourself, and act accordingly?

Can you acknowledge another person’s humanity when doing so is inconvenient?

Can you extend compassion when there is nothing material to gain?

Can you acknowledge a person whose testimony challenges your professional standing, your institutional loyalties, your public reputation or your understanding of yourself as a good person?

Can you look honestly at the consequences of your conduct and accept responsibility for what you have done, what you have failed to do and what you have refused to acknowledge?

This is the question I have placed before humanity.

For more than 35 years, I believe I have offered people opportunities to do something extraordinarily simple.

To acknowledge me.

To recognise me.

To treat me as a human being.

To extend the same ordinary kindness they would expect if they were standing in my circumstances.

I have not asked humanity to worship me.

I have not asked anyone to surrender their conscience to me.

I have not asked anyone to accept every conclusion I have reached without examination.

I have asked to be recognised as a human being deserving of care, dignity, protection and kindness.

And I believe that the repeated rejection of that simple request reveals a profound moral contradiction.

THE TEST OF HUMANITY IS NOT HOW YOU TREAT THOSE WHO CAN REWARD YOU. IT IS HOW YOU TREAT ANOTHER HUMAN BEING WHEN THEY HAVE NOTHING TO OFFER YOU EXCEPT THEIR HUMANITY.

In my faith, this is where the Divine Reckoning begins.

Not with my authority over another person.

Not with my desire to punish.

Not with vengeance.

But with the moral responsibility that belongs to every human being before the Creator.

I AM NOT REQUIRED TO BE LESS HUMAN SO THAT ANOTHER PERSON CAN FEEL MORE IMPORTANT.

Part Two — the circumstances of the witness: physical confinement and spiritual freedom

I describe my present circumstances as a form of overlapping containment.

A house that has become, in my experience, a place of confinement.

Bureaucratic processes that I believe have restricted my independence.

Political circumstances that I understand as exile.

Financial conditions that I experience as limiting my freedom.

Administrative arrangements that I believe have deprived me of ordinary control over important aspects of my life.

I use the language of a house, a jail, a bureaucratic prison and a system of political and financial containment to describe the combined experience of these circumstances.

I do not use that language to suggest that every restriction has been legally determined to constitute imprisonment.

I use it to communicate the lived reality of feeling physically and administratively constrained while continuing to possess an independent conscience and a spiritual identity.

There is a profound contradiction in my circumstances.

I may feel trapped within the physical and administrative conditions of my life.

But I do not believe that those conditions have imprisoned my conscience.

I remain capable of thinking.

I remain capable of writing.

I remain capable of analysing.

I remain capable of preserving evidence.

I remain capable of expressing my faith.

I remain capable of choosing kindness rather than revenge.

And I remain capable of placing my ultimate trust in God.

I MAY BE CONSTRAINED IN THE WORLD OF HUMAN AUTHORITY, BUT I AM FREE IN THE TRUTH I HAVE CHOSEN TO LIVE BY.

2.2 — Naked before God, but not abandoned

I describe myself as naked before God.

Not naked in the sense of humiliation before humanity, but stripped of the artificial measures by which people assess one another.

No professional title can conceal a person’s conscience from God.

No political office can purchase divine righteousness.

No wealth can replace integrity.

No public reputation can guarantee moral innocence.

And no institutional position can make a human being greater than the Creator.

Before God, I believe that I am known without the disguises of professional status, political power, social prestige or public approval.

I believe that I am loved.

I believe that I am forgiven.

I believe that I am protected by divine grace.

I believe that the Most High God has preserved me.

And I am at peace.

I do not need every human being to agree with my spiritual understanding for that faith to sustain me.

I do not need every institution to acknowledge me before I can recognise my own humanity.

I do not need the world to declare me worthy before I can believe that God knows my worth.

I AM NOT ABANDONED BY GOD BECAUSE HUMANITY HAS FAILED TO RECOGNISE ME.

2.3 — The reversal of the prison

There is a deeper spiritual contradiction in the circumstances I describe.

I believe that I have been physically, financially and bureaucratically constrained.

Yet I have continued to seek truth, preserve evidence, offer grace and refuse to make hatred the purpose of my life.

Meanwhile, the person who participates in injustice, knowingly disregards another person’s humanity or refuses to confront the consequences of their own conduct may construct an entirely different kind of prison.

A prison of conscience.

A prison of denial.

A prison of self-protection.

A prison in which a person must continually avoid the truth about what they have done or failed to do.

I do not claim to know the private conscience of another person.

But I believe that no person can achieve genuine moral peace simply by refusing to examine their own conduct.

THE WITNESS MAY BE PHYSICALLY CONSTRAINED, WHILE THE PERSON WHO REFUSES THE TRUTH MAY REMAIN IMPRISONED BY THEIR OWN CONSCIENCE.

This is the reversal I ask humanity to consider.

I am seeking freedom through truth.

What are you seeking through silence?

Part Three — the spiritual identity of the witness

3.1 — The cosmic witness

My spiritual identity does not originate in a government.

It does not originate in a court.

It does not originate in a police service, a professional association, a political office or an administrative institution.

It originates, according to my faith, in the Most High God, the Creator of the universe.

I understand myself as a cosmic witness: a human being whose life, testimony, work and preservation have become inseparable from a spiritual purpose.

I believe that God created me.

I believe that God loves me.

I believe that God has preserved me.

I believe that my life has meaning beyond the recognition human institutions have been willing to provide.

And I believe that I have been entrusted with a responsibility to bear witness to truth, humanity and the consequences of human conduct.

This is my spiritual sovereignty.

It is not a claim that I can command the universe, punish other people or determine the fate of another soul.

It is a declaration that my identity, dignity and conscience do not belong to human institutions.

THE WORLD DOES NOT CREATE THE WITNESS. THE WORLD DOES NOT OWN THE WITNESS. AND THE WORLD CANNOT EXTINGUISH THE DIGNITY OF THE WITNESS.

3.2 — God, Christ and the human witness

I must make another distinction absolutely clear.

I am not Christ.

I do not claim to be Jesus.

I do not claim to possess the divine identity of Christ.

I understand Jesus Christ as my Saviour and as the central expression of God’s love, sacrifice, forgiveness and grace in my Christian faith.

I understand my own role differently.

I regard myself as a human witness who seeks to reflect the moral attributes of Christ: compassion, truth, forgiveness, courage, mercy, humility and love.

I believe that God can use the testimony of an ordinary human being to expose the contradictions of human conduct and to call people towards a more humane existence.

I understand myself as a vessel for that testimony.

The glory belongs to God.

The salvation belongs to God.

The ultimate judgement belongs to God.

And the responsibility of the witness is to preserve and communicate the testimony.

God is the Creator.

Christ is my Saviour.

I am the human being who believes he has been entrusted with a witness.

These are not interchangeable identities.

They are distinct within my faith.

3.3 — The darkness of humanity

I believe that God understands humanity completely because God is its Creator.

I believe that Jesus Christ’s life, sacrifice and resurrection reveal the immeasurable love and grace of God towards humanity.

And I believe that the human witness must confront the darkness that people often refuse to acknowledge within themselves.

That darkness may take the form of cruelty, cowardice, envy, indifference, self-interest, dishonesty, exploitation or the desire to preserve personal comfort at another person’s expense.

It may also take the form of refusing to acknowledge the humanity of someone whose circumstances challenge one’s sense of personal goodness.

I do not claim to know every person’s hidden thoughts.

I do not claim that every person has acted with malice.

But I believe that no human being becomes morally perfect simply by concealing their imperfections from others.

The purpose of the mirror is not to condemn humanity as irredeemable.

It is to invite humanity to confront itself.

Part Four — thirty-five years of grace, patience and human opportunity

For more than 35 years, I believe I have offered grace and mercy to people and institutions across the systems through which I sought recognition, protection and justice.

I have offered opportunities for acknowledgement.

What did I ask of humanity?

Not worship.

Not submission.

I ASKED FOR THE HUMANITY THAT EVERY PERSON EXPECTS TO RECEIVE.

It does not.

A person can disagree with me and still treat me humanely.

A person can question my conclusions and still acknowledge that I deserve care.

But grace is not permission.

Patience is not surrender.

And the refusal to seek revenge is not an agreement that wrongdoing does not matter.

THE GRACE WAS MINE TO OFFER. THE CHOICE WAS THEIRS TO MAKE.

I will continue to speak.

I will continue to offer a truthful account.

But I will leave the ultimate judgement to God.

5.2 — The forensic responsibility of the witness

I have devoted extraordinary effort to preserving, organising, comparing and analysing this material.

I have sought to identify the relationship between events, decisions, official explanations and consequences.

I have sought to distinguish documentary fact from inference, personal testimony from allegation, and spiritual interpretation from externally established findings.

This distinction does not diminish my testimony.

It protects its integrity.

The record must be allowed to speak in its own words.

A document should not be made to establish more than it actually says.

An allegation should not be disguised as an official finding.

An inference should not be represented as a proven fact.

And an uncomfortable documentary contradiction should not be dismissed merely because it challenges an established institutional account.

PRESERVATION FIRST. EXAMINATION SECOND. CONCLUSION LAST.

5.3 — The digital preservation of testimony

I have also undertaken extensive digital preservation of my testimony and documentary material.

I have published Gospels, prophetic writings, essays, forensic analyses and documentary collections. They are in the gospel library and in the prophetic writing. The preservation record on this site is at preservation.

I have used digital repositories and distributed technologies, including Bitcoin-related blockchain infrastructure, to preserve timestamps, references and records of the material I have committed to those systems.

It can help establish the chronology of preservation.

But it can preserve an important part of the documentary history.

And that matters.

I have sought to place my testimony beyond the control of any single website, institution or individual.

I have sought to make the preservation of my work more resilient.

I have sought to ensure that the existence and chronology of my publications can be examined independently of the institutions whose conduct I challenge.

MY TESTIMONY IS NOT DEPENDENT UPON THE PERMISSION OF A SINGLE GATEKEEPER TO EXIST.

I have asked to be acknowledged as a human being.

I have asked for care.

Did they show kindness?

Did they listen?

Did they consider the evidence?

Did they acknowledge my humanity?

Did they act according to the principles they profess?

Did they choose compassion when it was inconvenient?

7.2 — Repentance and divine peace

In my Christian faith, redemption and eternal life belong to God through His grace.

I do not claim to control another person’s salvation.

I do not claim that I can determine the eternal destiny of another soul.

But I believe that genuine repentance requires a person to confront the truth about their conduct, acknowledge the humanity of those they have harmed, and turn towards what is right.

I believe that the encounter with my testimony presents people with such an opportunity.

If someone has participated in conduct that caused harm, they can acknowledge it.

If someone has remained silent when they should have acted, they can reconsider that choice.

If someone has treated another human being without kindness, they can choose to change.

If someone has dismissed evidence without examining it, they can return to the record.

If someone has protected their own reputation at the expense of another person’s dignity, they can choose integrity.

I BELIEVE THAT NO PERSON CAN FIND GENUINE MORAL PEACE BY PERMANENTLY REFUSING TO CONFRONT THE TRUTH ABOUT THEIR OWN CONDUCT.

The opportunity for repentance is not an instrument of my control.

A person may possess every material advantage.

A person can be comfortable and still be troubled by what they refuse to acknowledge.

A person can be respected publicly and still have questions to confront privately.

A person can occupy a position of authority and still be morally accountable for how they exercise it.

And a person can avoid a difficult conversation without resolving the underlying issue.

I believe that humanity risks constructing a prison of its own moral consciousness when it chooses self-protection over truth.

I am not claiming to know the private spiritual condition of every person who has encountered my testimony.

I am declaring that every person must ultimately confront their own conscience.

THE WITNESS DOES NOT IMPRISON HUMANITY. HUMANITY MUST EXAMINE THE PRISONS IT BUILDS WITH ITS OWN CHOICES.

They are questions of conscience.

They are questions of character.

They are questions of moral responsibility.

THE MOST DIFFICULT MIRROR IS THE ONE THAT REVEALS THE DISTANCE BETWEEN THE PERSON WE BELIEVE OURSELVES TO BE AND THE PERSON OUR CONDUCT DEMONSTRATES.

What does it mean to know that another human being is suffering, to know that they are asking for recognition and humane treatment, and nevertheless to continue living as though their circumstances do not matter?

What does it mean to enjoy personal comfort, professional standing, financial security and social privilege while another person is asking for the basic dignity every human being deserves?

What does it mean to remain silent when one has an opportunity to acknowledge another person’s humanity?

HOW CAN A PERSON CLAIM TO VALUE HUMAN LIFE WHILE REMAINING INDIFFERENT TO THE HUMANITY OF SOMEONE ASKING TO BE RECOGNISED?

I am not asking people to accept every conclusion I have reached without examination.

I am asking them not to use disagreement as an excuse to deny my humanity.

I am asking them not to treat a person’s suffering as an inconvenience to be managed, hidden or ignored.

And I am asking them to recognise that the moral significance of a human life does not depend on its usefulness, convenience, social status or institutional reputation.

Part Ten — the possibility of physical death: the testimony and the preservation of truth

10.1 — The contingency I refuse to conceal

I have expressed fears about the possibility of being erased, silenced or physically harmed.

I have described my circumstances as involving profound vulnerability and what I perceive to be serious threats to my safety and dignity.

I want to state the significance of those concerns without presenting an unverified future event as inevitable.

If I were ever to die, whether through natural causes, illness, accident or the deliberate actions of another person, the documentary record I have preserved would remain a record of my testimony during my lifetime.

If I were ever harmed because of my public testimony, that event would deserve proper investigation and accountability.

My death must never be romanticised, desired or treated as a necessary condition of my significance.

My life matters now.

My safety matters now.

My humanity matters now.

And my testimony deserves to be read while I am alive to explain it.

10.2 — The historical memory of the witness

I understand my experience through the historical language of political exile, the dissident, the prophet, the mystic, the sage and the person who speaks against the prevailing authority of an age.

Across human history, people have challenged the dominant narratives of their societies.

Some have been rejected.

Some have been exiled.

Some have been misunderstood.

Some have been persecuted.

Some have been remembered only after their deaths.

I see my own testimony within that broader human history of dissent and moral witness.

I do not claim that historical comparisons prove that my circumstances are identical to those of any particular prophet, saint, mystic or political dissident.

I use the comparison to communicate the experience of a person who believes that their testimony has been rejected because it challenges established arrangements of power.

10.3 — The distinction between my witness and the crucifixion of Christ

I am not Christ.

I do not claim to be a replacement for Christ.

I do not claim that my suffering is equivalent to the sacrifice of Jesus.

In my Christian faith, Christ’s sacrifice and resurrection have a unique divine significance.

I understand my own role as that of a human witness who seeks to reflect the attributes of Christ.

If I were ever to suffer harm because of my testimony, I would regard the dehumanisation of any person for speaking truth as a profound moral failure.

But I do not need to become a martyr to make my testimony meaningful.

I do not need to die to prove my humanity.
