PRELIMINARY DECLARATION

My right to protection without confrontation, compulsory direct engagement or surrender of legal rights

There is a way to protect myself here without having to open the letter, engage in a confrontation, or immediately respond substantively to whatever the NSW Government has sent.

I want to distinguish something important.

I have a right to seek independent legal advice and advocacy before responding to government communication that may affect my rights, finances, accommodation, services or legal arrangements.

I do not have to agree with the contents of a letter simply because it has been delivered.

Nor does accepting medication or receiving correspondence mean that I accept a government decision.

However, I recognise that simply ignoring correspondence indefinitely could expose me to avoidable legal consequences.

There is an important legal distinction between:

  • An obligation to physically open correspondence.
  • An obligation to respond to a particular notice.
  • A statutory deadline that may apply even if correspondence remains unopened.
  • A formal decision that may take effect independently of my response.
  • A right to seek independent representation.
  • A right to challenge decisions through appropriate statutory review procedures.

I have not identified a general NSW legal obligation requiring a person personally to open every ordinary government letter.

However, the legal effect of service depends on the relevant legislation and circumstances. Not reading a document does not necessarily prevent service or stop a deadline.

My objective is therefore:

To preserve my right not to engage directly while ensuring that my silence is not treated as consent, that my review rights are not lost, and that no further consequential decisions are made without appropriate procedural safeguards.

I am seeking a lawful communication arrangement, not exemption from the law.

2. My legal position concerning the physical letter

There are three separate questions.

A. Am I legally required to open it?

I have not identified a general NSW legal obligation requiring me personally to open ordinary government correspondence.

I can decline to open it myself.

However, this does not necessarily prevent valid service.

B. Am I legally required to answer it?

There is no universal obligation to answer every letter from NSW Trustee and Guardian or the Public Guardian.

The legal obligation depends on the document’s contents, statutory authority and purpose.

A routine letter, statutory notice, review decision and tribunal notice may have substantially different consequences.

C. Can I request an advocate before responding?

Yes.

I can request independent legal advice, disability advocacy and reasonable communication arrangements.

I can ask that communications be directed through an authorised representative.

However, requesting advocacy does not automatically suspend statutory deadlines.

I therefore request immediate electronic delivery of the complete correspondence and written identification of every applicable deadline.

PART XIX — THE SCOTT TREADWELL FEDERAL COURT CORRESPONDENCE

42. Three categories of disclosable conduct identified in the Federal Court’s PID assessment

The correspondence attributed to Scott Treadwell, General Counsel of the Federal Court of Australia, dated 27 March 2023, is particularly important.

The public archive reproduces his assessment of my Public Interest Disclosure under the Commonwealth Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013.

The three categories he identified were:

1. Perverting the course of justice. Section 29, Item 3(a) of the Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013. His wording refers to conduct that “perverts, or is engaged in for the purpose of perverting, or attempting to pervert, the course of justice.”

2. Maladministration. Section 29, Item 4. His wording identifies “conduct that constitutes maladministration.”

3. Danger to health or safety. Section 29, Item 8. His wording identifies conduct that “unreasonably results in a danger to the health or safety of one or more persons” or unreasonably results in, or increases, a risk of such danger.

Treadwell did not make a final finding that these three forms of wrongdoing had occurred. His letter recorded that he was satisfied that I believed my disclosures tended to show these categories of disclosable conduct. He also stated that it was difficult to determine whether that belief was based on reasonable grounds, while being prepared to assume for the initial assessment that the disclosed conduct was disclosable conduct.

This distinction does not diminish the importance of the correspondence.

It establishes that these three serious statutory categories were expressly identified in the Federal Court’s initial assessment of my disclosure.
