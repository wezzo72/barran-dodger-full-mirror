9. THE RECURRING ADMINISTRATIVE PATTERN: WHAT CAN BE TESTED

The ministerial appeal describes a recurring cycle: submission of documentary evidence; administrative acknowledgement; referral to another agency; rejection or limitation of complaint scope; failure to examine the underlying allegations; further correspondence; and continued absence of an effective remedy. The 35-Year Testimony similarly states that the author’s central grievance is the repeated receipt of referrals, administrative closures, silence or responses that do not address the substance of what he raised. This is the core analytical proposition of the archive.

To test the proposition, every complaint or disclosure must be coded by issue and outcome. The minimum fields are: date submitted; sender; recipient; reference number; alleged conduct; requested remedy; documents supplied; receipt acknowledged; legal or administrative basis invoked; scope accepted; enquiries actually undertaken; evidence considered; decision-maker; reasons; referral destination; review rights; final outcome; and whether the underlying issue was resolved. The same issue should then be followed across institutions. A referral should be counted as a referral, not as an investigation. A closure should be recorded with its stated basis, not automatically described as proof that no investigation occurred.

A pattern of similar outcomes may be evidence of a systemic problem even where there is no evidence of a coordinated conspiracy. Fragmented jurisdiction, rigid eligibility rules, poor record transfer, repeated requests for the same information, inadequate reasons or failure to identify a lead agency can produce a harmful cycle without central direction. Conversely, evidence of shared instructions, undisclosed communications,

common decision-making, inconsistent explanations or retaliatory treatment may support a more serious inference. The forensic report must test both possibilities and actively seek evidence that could disconfirm the author’s preferred explanation.

The strongest formulation supported at this stage is therefore: the archive contains a substantial, multi-agency documentary trail and the author reports that repeated administrative responses have not produced an effective remedy; the pattern warrants independent reconstruction and assessment. advocate to answer on the public’s behalf:

What do the institutions’ own records say about what was reported, what was received, what was assessed, what was decided, what was refused, what was referred elsewhere, and what was left unresolved?

The case should not be reduced to whether one official agrees with the account of the person who made the complaints. The relevant documentary questions are more concrete:

  • What did each agency receive, and on what date?
  • What did its own officers record or acknowledge?
  • What legal, administrative or policy framework did the agency say it was applying?
  • What did it investigate, and what did it expressly decline to investigate?
  • What reasons did it give for refusing, closing, restricting, referring or not allocating a matter?
  • Did a later institutional account accurately describe the earlier correspondence and decisions?
  • Were relevant records preserved, disclosed, withheld, or identified as unavailable?
  • Did the practical consequences for the complainant continue after the agency considered its own process complete?

These questions can be answered by examining the records themselves. They do not depend on accepting every interpretation advanced by the complainant. Equally, an agency’s decision to close a file, decline jurisdiction, reject a complaint or redirect a person elsewhere does not, by itself, establish that the underlying concerns were investigated or resolved.

The evidentiary rule: their words, their records, their decisions The strongest way to present this material is to place institutional records at the centre of the narrative. Each document must be examined in its own terms, with the following distinctions maintained throughout:

  • An original institutional record: a letter, email, decision, assessment, file note, formal notice or other document created or issued by an agency, court, police service, lawyer or official.
  • A record of receipt or process: an acknowledgement, reference number, allocation notice, referral, jurisdictional response or service restriction. It can establish that a process occurred, but not necessarily that the underlying allegation was investigated.
  • A decision and its reasons: what the decision-maker actually decided, the stated basis, the evidence identified and the review rights or limitations explained.
  • The complainant’s contemporaneous account: what Dr McLean said happened, what he reported, what he requested and what consequences he described at the time.
  • A later interpretation: the meaning or significance Dr McLean attributes to the documents when considered together.
  • An independently established finding: a conclusion made by a court, tribunal, statutory investigator or other body with authority to make that finding.

This distinction is not a concession to institutional power. It is what makes the report difficult to dismiss. It prevents a respondent from avoiding the actual documents by arguing that the report has confused an allegation with a finding. It also prevents an agency from presenting its own administrative response as proof that every issue raised was properly examined.

The governing principle is simple: a document proves what it records; its legal and factual significance must then be assessed against the full context. 3. The documentary significance of institutional acknowledgements A particularly important part of this report is the distinction between an institution acknowledging a communication and an institution resolving the matter.

An acknowledgement may establish:

  • That a communication reached an agency.
  • That a reference number was assigned.
  • That a particular office had responsibility for processing it.
  • That a decision or referral was made.
  • That the agency knew the complainant was seeking assistance.

It does not necessarily establish:

  • That the agency investigated the allegations.
  • That the agency accepted the complainant’s interpretation.
  • That the agency found misconduct.
  • That the underlying issue was resolved.
  • That the complainant received an effective remedy.

The framework: administrative fragmentation and the loss of a whole-person response Dr McLean’s writings use the concepts of social erosion, institutional mobbing, the psychology of erasure and administrative annihilation to describe the cumulative effect of institutional interactions.

The report can use those concepts as an analytical framework, while keeping the evidentiary question separate.

The framework asks whether a person’s ability to obtain assistance can be progressively undermined when:

  • Each agency considers only a narrow part of the person’s circumstances.
  • A complaint is redirected without an effective route to resolution.
  • A decision is made without addressing the practical consequences.
  • An information request is refused or delayed without a meaningful alternative.
  • A service provider’s records are not reconciled with the person’s account.
  • The person is required to repeat the same history to multiple institutions.
  • No institution accepts responsibility for examining the cumulative outcome.

This is the proposition that should be tested against the documents. It does not require the report to assert that every institution coordinated its conduct. A pattern of fragmented decisions and unresolved problems may be demonstrable without proving a common plan or conspiracy.

The central forensic question: isolated decisions or cumulative institutional failure?

The most important analytical question is whether the documentary history reveals a recurring sequence of administrative events that, considered cumulatively, produced substantial and avoidable harm.

The sequence described in the testimony can be expressed as follows:

  • McLean experiences or identifies an event that he believes involves misconduct, neglect, discrimination, financial detriment or a threat to his safety.
  • He communicates the concern to a public institution or service provider.
  • He supplies correspondence, records, supporting material or a formal complaint.
  • The recipient acknowledges, refers, assesses, declines, restricts or otherwise disposes of the matter.
  • McLean disputes the response, seeks further records or approaches another institution.
  • The original issue remains unresolved from his perspective.
  • Further administrative and practical consequences arise while the dispute continues.

The existence of such a sequence would be relevant to questions of procedural fairness, administrative accountability, disability rights and access to effective remedies.

However, the sequence must be demonstrated for each individual issue. A referral by one agency to another does not establish coordination. Five agencies independently receiving correspondence does not establish that they jointly agreed upon an outcome. A repeated classification may result from reliance on a common source, but that source and the actual information-sharing relationship must be identified.

The investigation must establish whether the repeated decisions were genuinely independent, whether one institution’s classification was adopted by another, whether an error was propagated, and whether any agency failed to correct information after receiving evidence that challenged it.

This is where the archive may have its greatest evidentiary value. 4. Chronological reconstruction and the institutional record 4.1 The claimed history beginning in 1990 McLean describes his experience as extending over approximately 35 years, beginning with psychiatric involvement in 1990.

Serious allegations: an independent verification protocol The testimony includes allegations concerning surveillance, entrapment, electronic interference, threats to kill, possible involvement of security agencies, deliberate targeting and possible attempts to cause physical harm.

These allegations are serious and should be recorded accurately as allegations. They should neither be accepted automatically nor dismissed without examination.

The archive itself identifies material that may be relevant, including police matter documents, IGIS/ASIO correspondence, recordings and the forensic research dossier on patented technologies.

The correct examination would distinguish five stages:

First: the reported experience. What did McLean personally observe, hear, receive or experience?

Second: the contemporaneous record. Is there an original recording, message, police event number, witness statement, medical record, photograph or other record created at the time?

Third: provenance. Can the date, author, device, speaker, recipient and completeness of the material be verified?

Fourth: interpretation. What does the material actually establish, and what alternative explanations remain possible?

Fifth: independent finding. Has a police investigation, court, regulator or other competent body reached a conclusion about the specific conduct?

A patent document may establish that a technology was described or patented. It does not prove that the technology was deployed against a particular person. A request for information to an intelligence oversight body does not establish that the agency conducted surveillance. A police complaint establishes that a report was made, not that every allegation

in the report was proven.

The appropriate standard is to test the evidence without either prejudgment or dismissal. 11. Preliminary findings The following findings are limited to the materials presently examined.
