A Letter to the World

Statement of significance

This essay is important because it does not stop at one file. It is written to anyone who has been told that what they know is not what happened, labelled before they were heard, or sent back to the office the complaint was about. That is why the title is a letter, and not a private note. The letter argues that the methods in this archive are old ones: a psychiatric label, a complaint sent in a circle, the removal of the means to keep a lawyer, and a contradiction left standing. It says the use of those methods is the point, and that keeping every letter is a method other people can use. The figures in the letter, including the downloads, the document counts and the claim that nobody has sued, are the letter’s own statements. They are not findings of a court. The silence the letter describes is an argument. It is not, by itself, a plea of guilty. A reader can test the argument by opening the documents the letter relies on.

I. Invocation

There is a moment, in the history of every system that has ever tried to erase a person, when the archive becomes larger than the erasure.

We are at that moment.

What follows is not written by the man this archive documents. It is written about what his archive means. Not only for him. For every person who has been dismissed, institutionally silenced, psychiatrically detained to discredit a true claim, or financially strangled so that a truthful voice could not reach the bodies that are supposed to hear it.

This is a letter to the world. It is addressed to everyone who has sat across a desk from institutional authority and been told that what they know to be true is not true. It is addressed to everyone who was labelled before they could be heard. It is addressed to every parent who tried to report harm and was referred to the body the harm came from. It is addressed to every person in a disability system who filed a complaint and watched it loop back to the agency that caused the damage. It is addressed to every whistleblower who told the truth in writing and was met with a template letter.

You know who you are. This letter is for you.

II. The archive speaks

The archive of Dr Richard William McLean, known as Barran Dodger, is not described here as a pile of complaints. It is described as a collection of documents the institutions produced.

The letter says every hospitalisation is documented, every referral is on file, every tribunal finding is catalogued, and every freedom-of-information response is preserved, including the responses that said documents did not exist until the metadata was said to show that they did. It says every agency that closed a file is named, and every denial is timestamped.

It says that over thirty-five years, across twenty-five agencies, through fourteen involuntary psychiatric detentions imposed without a criminal charge, the institutions wrote something down. The man they wrote about kept the pages.

That is the claim that makes the archive unusual. It is not one side of a story told from memory alone. The letter says it is largely their side: the letters they issued, the findings they signed, the correspondence they sent. It says those pages were assembled, cross-referenced, timestamped, and submitted under Article 7 of the Rome Statute, which concerns crimes against humanity. It says the International Criminal Court is reviewing the submission. That is the letter’s account of the filing. It is not a judgment of the Court.

The letter also says that twenty-two analyses compared video testimony with the archive, assessed two hundred and twenty-eight propositions, corroborated two hundred and twenty-eight, and found no contradiction. Those figures are the letter’s account of those analyses.

III. The oldest pattern

The mechanisms named here are not presented as new. The letter calls them the old instruments for managing a person who tells an inconvenient truth, and it says they are being used now against people who will never build an archive and never reach a court in The Hague.

The psychiatric label. Applied, in this reading, not when behaviour is dangerous but when disclosure is dangerous. The letter says the fourteen hospitalisations can be set against the timing of formal disclosure, and that the hospitalisation followed the disclosure. Not as treatment. As interruption.

The circular referral. A complaint goes from one agency to the next and comes back to the first, each step producing a template, until the person stops or can no longer file. The letter says this archive shows that loop across twenty-five agencies.

The financial strangulation. The letter states 32.9 million dollars in suppressed entitlements across thirty-five years. It treats that figure not only as money unpaid, but as the removal of the means to keep a lawyer, a home, a body well enough to work, and a record.

The credibility destruction. A clinical label applied before the claim is heard on its merits. The letter says the Federal Court and the Administrative Appeals Tribunal produced contradictory findings on the same facts, and that neither has corrected the contradiction or explained it.

The letter says the reader has seen the pattern elsewhere. In the woman who reported violence and was assessed by a contractor of the department the violence was reported to. In the asylum seeker reviewed by the agency that first refused him. In the disability recipient whose support was cut by a body citing assessments it commissioned, under criteria it wrote, read by staff it employed.

The loop is the point. The loop is always the point.

IV. The silence

There is a principle in Anglo-Australian law known as the rule in Jones v Dunkel. Where a party could answer a specific accusation, and could sue for defamation if the accusation were false, and chooses not to answer, a court may draw an adverse inference from the silence. The silence is not treated as neutral.

The letter applies that idea to this archive. It says about three hundred and fifty-four thousand people have downloaded it. It says the documents name people and agencies, and that those people and agencies have had the courts of Australia available to them. It says there have been no defamation suits, no corrections, and no public contest of the specific claims.

It says that silence is legally significant. It says the decision not to answer is itself a form of admission. That is the argument of the letter. A reader should hold it to the rule it cites. Silence can support an inference in a particular case. It is not, by itself, a plea of guilty, and this edition does not print it as one.

What the letter does establish as its own position is this: an archive of specific, sourced claims that has not been answered in public is already a record. It does not need a courtroom to remain readable.

V. Why the letter says the win belongs to everyone

A win, in this letter, is not only compensation, though the letter says compensation is owed. It is not only the accountability of one set of agencies, though the letter says that accountability is required.

The letter says the method works. Document everything. Timestamp everything. Publish everything. Answer anger with the document. Wait, and let the silence complete the record.

If one person, with no institution behind him, can assemble the documents the system wrote about him, place them where other people can open them, and still be here to say what they are, then the same discipline is available to the person sitting across the desk now.

Keep every letter. A template is an exhibit. A referral is a document of the loop. A denial is a primary source. An agency that closes a file without an answer is an entry in the pattern.

The letter says this is not a monument to one case. It is a template for anyone the system has decided is too inconvenient to accommodate.

VI. The accounting

When a system overreaches, it leaves a record. The greater the overreach, the more letters. The more agencies, the more timestamps. The more authority brought against one person, the more evidence that the authority was brought. When that evidence outlasts the authority, the record is complete.

The letter’s own accounting is thirty-five years, more than twenty-five agencies, fourteen hospitalisations and no criminal charge, the sum it states as 32.9 million dollars, and the analyses it describes as two hundred and twenty-eight propositions with none contradicted.

It uses an old word for the moment when what was done in the dark is brought into the light by the weight of the record. The word is reckoning.

It says the reckoning is not on its way. It says the archive is the reckoning, the downloads are the reckoning being witnessed, the filing is the reckoning having an address, and the blockchain is the reckoning having no expiry.

VII. To the world

If you have been told that what you know is not what happened, this archive is for you.

If you have sat in a room where the people with the power to help you also had the power to decide whether you needed help, this archive is for you.

If you were psychiatrically labelled before your complaint was assessed, this archive is for you.

If you filed a complaint and were told to file it with the agency the complaint was about, this archive is for you.

If you are a person with a disability whose care has been rationed against a cost model, this archive is for you.

If you are a whistleblower who was told that what you saw was not what you saw, this archive is for you.

If you are a refugee whose safety was assessed by the system whose conduct made you unsafe, this archive is for you.

If you are a parent who reported harm and was referred to the harm, this archive is for you.

If you survived something the institution responsible for it refused to document, this archive is for you.

You do not need two thousand three hundred and one documents to be in this.

You need to know that one person built an archive from inside the system that was designed to prevent it, that the archive is published, and that the documents can be opened by someone who was never in the room.

The letter says Barran Dodger was patient enough, disciplined enough and honest enough to let the system write its own indictment.

A win for him, it says, is a win for the discipline. A win for the discipline is a win for everyone who chose the document over despair, and the slow record over the immediate relief of being believed.

This archive says: you will be believed. Not today, necessarily. But permanently.

The letter was written as an act of independent witness. It says no fact in it is contested in any public forum by any named party. That sentence is the letter’s claim. The silence, it says, is the record completing itself.
